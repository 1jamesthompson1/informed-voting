Current bills & flashpoints

What’s actually in front of Parliament.

Election campaigns are loud, but laws are where policy becomes real. This is a plain-language guide to a selection of recent and current bills that matter for the 2026 election — including the ones that are already law, the ones still being debated, and one that was defeated.

Status checked 5 November 2026. Bills move quickly; always check the Parliament or Legislation website for the latest version. This is a selection, not a complete list.

◉
How a bill becomes law

A bill is a proposed law. It usually goes through a first reading, a select committee (where the public can submit), a second reading, a committee of the whole House, and a third reading. Then it needs Royal assent. A bill can be amended, delayed or defeated at any stage. “In force” means it is already law; “before Parliament” means it is still being debated.

Before Parliament Government bill · 346—1

References to Tiriti o Waitangi/Treaty of Waitangi (Standardisation) Amendment Bill

What it does: Standardises the wording of Treaty references across a range of Acts, including climate, education, health, local government and data legislation. The Government says it keeps references to Treaty principles and does not change their legal effect.

Why it matters: It is the current Treaty-related bill after the Treaty Principles Bill was defeated. It comes from the National–NZ First coalition agreement to review Treaty references. Critics say it could still weaken Treaty obligations in practice; supporters say it reduces uncertainty.

Related survey area: Te Tiriti & democracy

Reported from select committee Government bill · 251—2

Electoral (District Boundaries) Amendment Bill

What it does: Decouples electoral boundary reviews from the census and moves them to every second electoral cycle. It would keep the number of Māori electorates at seven until at least 2032, even though the Māori roll has grown enough for an eighth seat.

Why it matters: It affects the number of Māori seats and the voting power of everyone on the Māori roll. Labour, the Greens and Te Pāti Māori oppose it; the Government says it is needed because the census is moving to an annual model.

Related survey area: Te Tiriti & democracy

Member’s bill · before Parliament 213—1

Deepfake Digital Harm and Exploitation Bill

What it does: Expands the definition of an “intimate visual recording” to include images or videos created or altered with AI to show a person in an intimate context without their consent. It updates both the Crimes Act and the Harmful Digital Communications Act.

Why it matters: It is one of the few AI-specific bills before Parliament. It targets sexually explicit deepfakes, not political deepfakes or AI safety more broadly.

Related survey area: AI & technology

Read the bill on Legislation.govt.nz
Passed / phasing in Government bill · 313

Social Security (Modernisation) Amendment Bill

What it does: Expands the Ministry of Social Development’s ability to use automated electronic systems to make decisions about benefits and obligations. Most provisions were due to take effect from 1 July 2026.

Why it matters: It is a live example of AI and automated decision-making in public services. The Human Rights Commission and the Greens warned about the risk of errors and a lack of human review; the Government says it improves efficiency.

Related survey areas: Welfare · AI & technology

Read the bill on Legislation.govt.nz
In force Act · amended 2025

Fast-track Approvals Act 2024

What it does: Creates a fast-track consenting process for major projects. An expert panel considers projects and ministers make the final decision. It covers infrastructure, housing, mining, energy and other development.

Why it matters: It is one of the most contested environmental laws in recent years. Supporters say it cuts delays; critics say it reduces public consultation and environmental scrutiny. It is directly relevant to mining, energy and housing proposals.

Related survey areas: Climate · Housing · Transport

Read the official overview
In force Act · from 21 November 2024

Gangs Act 2024

What it does: Bans the display of gang insignia in public places, gives police dispersal powers and allows courts to impose non-consorting orders. It also gives greater weight to gang membership at sentencing.

Why it matters: It is the law behind the gang patch ban. Labour now says it will keep the ban if elected; the Greens and Te Pāti Māori oppose it. The Act is a live test of whether tougher enforcement reduces harm or pushes it elsewhere.

Related survey area: Crime & justice

Read the police overview
In force Act · from 20 December 2025

Electoral Amendment Act 2025

What it does: Ends same-day enrolment (the deadline is now 13 days before election day), bans prisoners from voting, raises the anonymous donation disclosure threshold, and restricts food and drink near polling places.

Why it matters: It changes who can vote and how easy it is to enrol. Labour, the Greens and Te Pāti Māori called it voter suppression; the Government says it makes the electoral system more robust.

Related survey area: Te Tiriti & democracy

Read the Ministry of Justice summary
In force Act · 18 September 2026

Local Government (System Improvements) Amendment Act 2026

What it does: Refocuses the statutory purpose of local government on cost-effective infrastructure, public services and regulation. It also changes governance and performance rules for councils.

Why it matters: It affects rates, local democracy and how councils set priorities. Supporters say it improves accountability and focuses councils on core services; critics say it narrows the role of local government and reduces community voice.

Related survey area: Te Tiriti & democracy

Member’s bill · not passed NZ First

Māori Seats Referendum Bill

What it does: Would require a binding referendum on the future of the Māori electorates, to be held alongside a general election.

Why it matters: It would put a constitutional question about Māori representation to a simple majority vote. NZ First supports it; Labour, the Greens and Te Pāti Māori oppose it.

Related survey area: Te Tiriti & democracy

Member’s bill · not passed NZ First

Display of Flags (Government Premises) Bill

What it does: Would restrict flags on Crown buildings and local authority property to the official New Zealand flag, with limited exceptions.

Why it matters: It is a flashpoint over free expression, rainbow inclusion and the place of the tino rangatiratanga flag. NZ First supports it; the Greens and Te Pāti Māori oppose it.

Related survey area: Te Tiriti & democracy

Defeated Second reading · 10 April 2025

Treaty Principles Bill

What it did: Would have defined the principles of the Treaty of Waitangi in statute, with a focus on equal individual rights and parliamentary sovereignty. It was voted down 112–11 at its second reading.

Why it matters: It triggered the largest protests in recent New Zealand history and remains a defining political issue. The current Standardisation Bill is a separate, narrower approach to Treaty references.

Related survey area: Te Tiriti & democracy

No overarching law AI regulation

AI regulation in New Zealand

What exists: New Zealand has no overarching AI Act. It relies on existing privacy, human rights, consumer and criminal law, plus the Deepfake Digital Harm and Exploitation Bill and the automated decision-making changes in social security.

Why it matters: AI is being used in public services, workplaces and political content, but there is no single regulator or safety-testing regime. Labour and the Greens propose an AI office; the Greens go further with a dedicated AI plan and data-centre limits. National and ACT focus on adoption and innovation.

Related survey area: AI & technology
