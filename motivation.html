Why this exists

A good voter is not a voter who agrees with you.

The goal is not to make everyone vote the same way. It is to make it easier to vote with an accurate picture of what the parties actually propose — and to respect people who reach a different conclusion.

This project started with a simple frustration: it is hard to know what you are voting for. Party brands are loud. Policy detail is scattered across manifestos, press releases, interviews and news reports. By the time you have compared two parties on one issue, you have forgotten what the first one said. And the loudest voices are often the least interested in helping you understand the trade-offs.

It also started with a question that is easy to state and hard to answer: what makes someone a good voter? Not a voter who agrees with me. A voter I can respect, even when I think they are wrong.

The good voter

The best framing I have found comes from a note I wrote to myself, which became a public blog issue called “The good voter”. It distinguishes between a voter you agree with and a voter you can respect:

A good voterA voter you agree with
Has a model of the world that is broadly correct. If I vote for Party A, it increases the chance they will get in. Has a model that agrees with you. We both agree that taking vaccines gives you autism.
Has accurate information about the world. Party A is focused on climate policy; Party B wants to reduce tax. Shares the same world view as you. Thinks Party C is full of evil green lizards.
Has values that take seriously the wellbeing of other people, and of future generations. Shares the same value set as you, whatever it is.

This is not a test that anyone passes perfectly. It is a direction of travel. A good voter can be wrong about a policy, or disagree with me about values, and still be doing the work: trying to understand the world, checking the information, and weighing the interests of people who are not in the room.

“The purpose of voting is like an ensemble machine. Everyone has a slightly different model, information and values. If we ask them all, we get something more accurate.”

Paraphrased from “The good voter”, 1jamesthompson1.github.io

Why information is the place to start

We cannot change each other’s values by building a website. We can make the information better. A voter who knows what each party has actually committed to is harder to fool, harder to polarise, and more likely to vote for reasons they can defend.

The problem is not that people are stupid. It is that the information environment is hostile to understanding. Policy is buried. Party branding is designed to trigger identity. News coverage focuses on the horse race, the gaffe and the leader. And the sheer volume of material means that even a motivated voter runs out of time.

Clear Vote is a response to that specific problem. It does not tell you what to value. It tries to give you a cleaner, more comparable picture of what the parties propose, with the sources attached, so you can apply your own values to it.

Why the survey is blind

If you see the party name first, it is hard not to react to the brand. You may give a policy a fairer hearing because it comes from a party you like, or dismiss it because it comes from a party you do not. That is not a moral failing; it is how human beings work. The blind survey changes the order: policy first, party second.

This does not remove bias. It does not make the exercise neutral. It simply gives you a chance to notice what you actually think about a proposition before you know who is proposing it. Then, on the audit page, you can see the names and check whether your reaction changes.

Why pluralism matters

A healthy democracy is not one where everyone agrees. It is one where people can disagree without treating each other as enemies, and where the losing side still has a stake in the system. That requires more than tolerance. It requires a willingness to believe that the other person might have a point, and that the country is better when decisions are tested by disagreement.

This project is deliberately non-partisan. It does not endorse a party. It does not assume that one side is good and the other is evil. It tries to describe what each party wants in plain language, including the parts that are uncomfortable or expensive, and then lets you decide.

What this tool does — and does not do

It does

  • Compare published 2026 policy positions across seven parties.
  • Hide party names while you answer, to reduce brand bias.
  • Show where you agree and differ, issue by issue.
  • Explain MMP and show a polling snapshot of how votes become seats.
  • Link every non-null position to a source for auditing.
  • Work on a phone, print cleanly, and keep your answers on your device.

It does not

  • Tell you how to vote.
  • Predict the election result or the next government.
  • Measure competence, honesty, character or trust.
  • Capture every issue or every party.
  • Replace the parties’ full manifestos or the news.
  • Collect your data, run ads, or accept donations.

Why MMP makes the choice harder

New Zealand’s mixed-member proportional system gives you two votes: a party vote and an electorate vote. The party vote usually decides the shape of the government, because it determines the share of seats each party gets. But because no party usually wins a majority alone, the government is formed through negotiation. That means a vote for a party is also a vote for the coalition possibilities that party enables.

This is why two voters with almost identical policy views can reasonably vote differently. One may want to express their closest match. Another may want to support the bloc they would rather see govern. A third may want to give a small party more leverage in the negotiations. None of these is automatically irrational. The results page lays out these three lenses, with the current polling numbers, and lets you decide which one fits your values.

The one thing it will not do is pretend that the choice is simple. It is not. That is why it is worth taking seriously.

A note on the limits of models

Polls are not predictions. Coalition maths is not destiny. A manifesto is not a binding contract. A party’s position can change after the election, and individual MPs can vote differently on conscience issues. The survey is a snapshot of published positions on 4 November 2026, not a forecast of what a government would do.

That is also why the audit page matters. The point is not to trust the percentage. The point is to be able to check it. If you disagree with a coding, the source is there. If you think a statement is unfair, the evidence is there. If you find a mistake, the correction process is there.

What you can do next

Take the survey. Read the sources. Talk to someone who disagrees with you. Vote.
